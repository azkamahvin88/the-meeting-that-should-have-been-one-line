# Design — The Meeting That Should Have Been One Line

## What the brief teaches (studied before writing anything)

- Blind round: leaderboard withheld, so no top-10 study is possible. The study
  below is built from the judge brief itself instead of from other entries.
- Judge persona (verbatim): "a deadpan office satirist who has survived more
  pointless meetings than anyone should."
- The judge compares entries pairwise and weighs, most important first:
  1. A single line, one sentence that could have replaced an entire pointless
     meeting, unmistakably office humor.
  2. Kills the meeting outright: after this line, no one could justify
     holding the meeting at all.
  3. Relatable to anyone who has sat through a useless meeting, drawn from
     real office life.
  4. Lands as a joke, with a turn or a sting that makes it funny rather than
     merely true.
  5. Stands alone as a single line, needing no context or explanation.
- Tie-breaks, in order: more playoff games, then **shorter text (fewer
  bytes)**, then earlier receipt. So the line must be as short as possible
  while still satisfying criteria 1–5 fully.
- Hard gates: exactly one sentence (ends with ".", no ". " inside, no "?"
  or "!"), 1–200 characters, stands alone. No throat-clearing ("Imagine…",
  "What if…"), no context-dependent references ("as we discussed yesterday"),
  no generic complaint ("this meeting is boring") that leaves the meeting
  justifiable.
- Entries reproducing a house rung (same bytes, or same words after case +
  whitespace normalisation) get no prize: every candidate below is written
  from scratch by me, in my own words.

## What I learned from the brief (the angles that matter)

The strongest lines do not describe the meeting — they **remove its reason
to exist**. The kill is strongest when the decision is already final AND
written down (criterion 2), because a written approval cannot be re-opened
by a meeting. The sting is strongest when the waste is priced in human
terms (salaries, an hour of life) rather than abstract ("waste of time").
Relatability comes from universal office facts: unread emails, threads
everyone already agreed in, recurring invites nobody remembers creating.
The line must carry its own setup and punch in one sentence, with no
"as you know" or "like last time" crutch (criterion 5).

## Why #1 wins

- **C1 (5):** it IS the replacement line — the first half states the finished
  decision, the second half states what the meeting would pointlessly do
  with it. Unmistakably office humor (email, salaries, meeting).
- **C2 (5):** "already approved in writing" is the hardest kill in the list.
  A written approval is final and external to the room; no one in the
  meeting can re-open it, so no one can justify holding the meeting. This
  beats #2/#9 (a "yes" in a thread can be revisited) and #7/#22 (a meeting
  about scheduling still has a nominal purpose).
- **C3 (5):** everyone has sat in a meeting that re-read an email nobody
  read. The unread-email ritual is the most universal office experience in
  the list, more universal than #3 (needs a client) or #16 (needs a shipped
  feature).
- **C4 (4):** the sting is "thirty salaries listening to that email read
  aloud" — the waste is priced in people and payroll, and the image of
  grown professionals being read an email is deadpan-funny. It loses one
  point only because #2/#9 land a slightly sharper one-liner turn ("say yes
  a second time", "pretend this meeting was necessary"), but those lose on
  C2, which the judge weighs first.
- **C5 (5):** fully self-contained — decision, approval, email, salaries,
  meeting — no prior context, no names, no "as discussed".
- **Tie-break:** 126 characters, well inside the 90–160 target and shorter
  than most rivals at the same score level (#3 is 122 but scores lower on
  C3/C4; the 120-char #2 scores lower on C2).
